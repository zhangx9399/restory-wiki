## How Device Selling Fits the Shop Loop

ReStory presents a repair-shop cycle rather than a stand-alone trading puzzle. The official Steam description says you run a shop, care for finances, browse a Y2K-style web for spare parts, restore devices, and fulfill in-person and online orders. Selling a device therefore belongs after inspection, repair, reassembly, and a final check. Money received is only one side of the decision; the cash and parts committed earlier matter too.

Use a simple working record for each device: where it came from, what you paid, which parts you replaced, and the sale value the game currently displays. This is a decision aid, not a hidden-mechanics claim. A number observed in one demo save, patch, or player post can change and should not be copied into a permanent rule.

## Checking a Device Before Buying or Repairing

Before committing money, inspect every detail the current interface lets you see. Identify the device model, visible state, available description, and displayed cost. Then check whether the parts you may need are available through the in-game store and whether buying them would consume money needed for active customer work. If information is incomplete, treat that uncertainty as a cost rather than filling the gap with a guess.

The June marketplace discussion shows why caution matters. Players reported listings that did not match expectations. A developer first acknowledged a demo-era balancing issue, then later said the full release had rebalanced the marketplace and request frequency. These version-labeled replies do not prove that any seller rating or description predicts damage today. Seller stars are not a stable universal guarantee, and listing text is not a stable universal guarantee of condition or profit.

## Tracking Parts and Repair Costs

Write down the acquisition amount first. Add each purchased replacement part when you install it. If the interface displays another device-specific fee, include it as well. Keep optional shop upgrades and unrelated customer jobs separate, so the result describes this device instead of the whole day. Your basic comparison is displayed sale value minus recorded device costs, with a safety buffer for anything uncertain.

Do not confuse inventory you already own with a free input. A spare part still has value because using it here means it cannot be used elsewhere. You can note that opportunity cost separately if the game does not show a replacement price. The purpose is consistency, not accounting precision: no exact pricing formula is exposed in the cited official sources.

## Preparing a Device for Sale

Finish the physical repair before evaluating the listing. Recheck that removed components are back in place, fasteners are accounted for, and cleaned or replaced parts appear correctly seated. Use any testing or completion feedback offered by your current build. If the game still indicates a fault, return to inspection rather than assuming the market will price the device as complete.

Cosmetic work may be enjoyable, and official demo news documents device painting with an Airbrush and palettes. It does not establish a universal resale bonus. Customize because you want the result or because the current game explicitly signals a benefit, not because an older screenshot or anecdote appears to promise one. A repaired condition is not a stable universal guarantee of one particular return.

## Comparing Cost, Condition, and Sale Value

Make the decision from three columns: recorded cost, currently observed condition, and currently displayed sale value. If the sale value does not comfortably exceed the costs you can verify, wait, reassess, or use your money elsewhere. Purchase cost is not a stable universal guarantee of later value; a cheap device can require more work, while a costly one can still be a poor trade.

The safest conclusion is comparative: one option appears better than another under the values visible now. It is not predictive. Time spent repairing, parts availability, and cash needed for open orders may change which choice is sensible. Preserve enough funds to continue ordinary shop work instead of placing the whole budget behind one uncertain listing.

## A Safe Profit Checklist

1. Read every detail the current listing or device screen actually shows.
2. Record the acquisition amount before beginning work.
3. Check part availability and preserve funds for active customer orders.
4. Add each replacement part and displayed device-specific expense.
5. Reassemble carefully and use current testing feedback where available.
6. Compare the current sale value with your recorded total.
7. Leave a buffer for uncertainty instead of targeting a break-even result.
8. Treat seller claims and player examples as clues, never universal rules.
9. Recheck the numbers after a patch or when the interface changes.

This checklist reduces avoidable mistakes; it cannot guarantee profit. No exact sale formula is officially confirmed, and live values in your build take priority over this guide.
